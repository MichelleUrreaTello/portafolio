Case Study

Activity Pass & Dynamic Itinerary

A full research-to-prototype process: understanding why travelers hesitate to book activities online, then designing a flexible solution around it.

User ResearchMVP PrioritizationUsability Testing
Role

UX Researcher & Designer

Company

Despegar — Boarding Pass UX Program

Timeline

2023

Status

Completed prototype

Overview

Despegar wanted to shift part of its business from pure booking toward destination advice and inspiration — helping travelers plan the activities around their trip, not just flights and hotels. The business goal was to make the app a place travelers associate with inspiration for their dream trip, not only the place to book it. This project was my final capstone in Despegar's internal "Boarding Pass" UX training program, targeting travelers in Argentina and Brazil who buy travel packages and want advice on activities, on mobile.

The Challenge

The open question was simple to state and hard to answer: what would actually make someone comfortable buying activities from the website ahead of time, instead of waiting to decide once they'd already arrived at their destination?

Research

Interview Design

I conducted five qualitative interviews using a guide structured in five stages: introduction and welcome, icebreaker questions, general questions about past travel experiences, questions specific to activity planning, and a closing space for open comments. Sample questions included: "What motivates you to go online to plan your trip?", "How flexible are you when planning your trip's activities?", and "What do you take into account when building your travel itinerary?"

Who I Talked To

Participants were 25–40 years old, based in Buenos Aires, traveling as couples with children. They travel at least once a year for 15+ days, and their routine is demanding — several worked medical shifts. Their goals: relax, experience new cultures, book their whole trip in one place, and find good deals.

What They Told Me

“I like being able to buy everything together. On my last trip — 15 days in Miami — I wanted to see new places, like the parks.”

— Juli

“When I traveled alone I just went wherever I wanted. Traveling with my kids, we look for a balance everyone enjoys — museums, food, activities for the kids.”

— Mariano

“For activities, weather matters, and how far it is from where I'm staying. If I'm tired, I'd rather just rest.”

— Ignacio

Findings

Usage patterns

  • Little time to plan — they check deals before requesting time off work.
  • Planning shifts depending on who they travel with (family, partner, kids) due to time and budget.
  • They favor nature and mountain destinations for calm and family-friendly variety.
  • They lean toward packages because it simplifies planning and tends to get better deals.

Needs

  • Interested in detailed recommendations to build their itinerary with confidence.
  • Prefer buying activities at the destination itself — it lets them adjust plans and find better prices.
  • Price, distance to lodging, and weather are the top details they look for.

Benchmark

Prior research into user motivations reinforced the direction: 69% of users said they'd rather spend more money on better activities than on a nicer hotel room. Detailed product information proved to be a high-impact advisory driver, alongside references, reviews, and pre-purchase reassurance.

Defining the Solution

How Might We

"How might we build trust with travelers going with their families to buy their activities inside our app?"

MVP Prioritization (MoSCoW)

Must

  • Personalized search — matched to trip type and interests.
  • A dynamic itinerary, editable both before and during the trip.
  • "Activity Pass": a bundle of suggested activities for the trip period.
  • Reviews and ratings to build trust in flexibility and service.

Should

  • A "sales advisor" chat during checkout to resolve doubts.
  • A vouchers / booking-confirmation section.
  • An FAQ section.

Information Architecture

Starting from the "Activity Pass" category, the structure branches into three main paths:

Filters

  • Trip type
  • Categories
  • Services included
  • Budget
  • Most popular
  • Most recommended

About the Activity Pass

  • Recommended Activity Passes
  • Activity Pass detail
  • Description & reviews
  • Included activities — photos, description, inclusions/exclusions, location, duration, activity reviews

Dynamic Itinerary

  • Activity Pass activities — photo, duration, location, date & time
  • Calendar within the travel period

Prototype

The Figma prototype brings the intended flow to life: users can personalize their search, review an Activity Pass, build an itinerary, and confirm it while keeping the trip flexible.

Usability Testing

I ran an exploratory usability evaluation with two participants to identify comprehension and flow issues in the prototype. The findings below informed iteration; they are directional rather than statistically conclusive.

What Worked

  • The itinerary confirmation flow was completed successfully by all participants.
  • Users found the relevant information about each Activity Pass — title, price, offer, description, duration, and map stood out.
  • 1 of 2 users understood that the itinerary could still be edited during the trip.

What Didn't

“It says Activity Pass, but I don't understand the label — I already know I'm inside Activity Pass.”

“It feels like I'm being shown a new product again, inside one I already picked.”

2 of 2 users didn't understand the "dynamic itinerary" wording, and had questions about what exactly was included in the selected Activity Pass.

What I Changed

Following heuristic review, I iterated: added bottom navigation for system visibility, reordered the search flow to lead with destination and dates (matching the real-world mental model), added an "I haven't decided my dates yet" option for user control, added green confirmation states once a date was set, and added progress bars, close options, and "skip" CTAs to modals.

Iterations supporting error prevention, visibility of system status, and user control.

Iterations improving search hierarchy, flexibility, and navigation visibility.

Outcome

The project concluded as a complete design deliverable: a functional prototype backed by a documented design system, informed by qualitative interviews and iterated through an exploratory usability pass. It was a portfolio and training-program project, not a production launch.

Reflection

This is the case study I'd point to as my strongest example of rigorous, end-to-end process — interviews through synthesis, prioritization, prototyping, and testing, all in one project. It's the bar I want to bring into a larger organization, where the stakes (and the complexity) are higher.
